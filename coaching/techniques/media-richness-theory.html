Media Richness Theory

A communication theory proposing that channels differ in richness and should be matched to the ambiguity or equivocality of the task.

Core idea: Choose communication channels intentionally instead of assuming either async text or meetings are always superior.

Understand the method first

Lower ambiguity

Stable facts, routine updates and reference information can often use lean asynchronous media effectively.

Higher ambiguity

Novel, emotionally charged or equivocal issues benefit from richer interaction, faster feedback and the ability to clarify meaning.

Lineage: Richard Daft and Robert Lengel developed Media Richness Theory in the 1980s. Richer media provide more immediate feedback and cues; leaner media can be efficient for clearer information.

How to use it

  1. Clarify whether the communication need is to inform, align, decide, negotiate or resolve conflict.
  2. Estimate ambiguity, emotional stakes and need for immediate feedback.
  3. Choose a channel rich enough for the task without creating unnecessary meeting load.
  4. After rich synchronous discussion, capture the resulting decision or agreement in a durable channel.

Coach with it

Use the method as a lens for inquiry, not as a rigid prescription. Useful coaching questions include:

“Is this problem clear enough for async text?”

“How quickly do misunderstandings need to be corrected?”

“Are emotion or relationship dynamics important to the decision?”

“What needs to be documented after the conversation?”

Agile example

A routine release status update is posted asynchronously. A high-stakes architecture disagreement moves to a synchronous discussion with shared visuals, then the decision is documented afterward.

Watch for

Watch for

Face-to-face is not automatically best for every task.

Watch for

Accessibility, time zones, inclusion and documentation needs also shape channel choice.
