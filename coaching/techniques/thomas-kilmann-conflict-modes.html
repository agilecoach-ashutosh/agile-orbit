Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Modes

A practical model for choosing how to respond when your concerns and another person's concerns appear incompatible. The model describes five conflict-handling modes using two dimensions: assertiveness and cooperativeness.

Core idea: The model does not rank one style as universally best. All five modes can be useful. The skill is to choose a mode deliberately based on the situation rather than using a favourite response out of habit.

The two dimensions

VERTICAL AXIS

Assertiveness

How strongly you try to satisfy your own concerns, needs or position in the conflict.

HORIZONTAL AXIS

Cooperativeness

How strongly you try to satisfy the other person's concerns, needs or position in the conflict.

The TKI is an assessment instrument as well as a model. Agile Orbit explains the model and how to coach with it; it does not reproduce the proprietary assessment questionnaire.

The five modes — in detail

1. CompetingASSERTIVE · UNCOOPERATIVE

What it means. You pursue your own concern decisively. The emphasis is on getting your position, decision or protection need met even if the other side's concern is not met at that moment.

Useful when.

  • Urgent action is required and there is little time for extended discussion.
  • The issue is much more important to you or to the system than it is to the other party.
  • A clear boundary, policy, safety requirement or non-negotiable constraint must be protected.
  • A decision has to be made after reasonable consultation has already happened.

Agile example. During a production incident, the incident commander may make an immediate rollback decision rather than trying to build full consensus while customer impact continues.

Risk of overuse. People may stop contributing, hide disagreement or experience decisions as power plays.

2. CollaboratingASSERTIVE · COOPERATIVE

What it means. Both sides' concerns are treated as important. The goal is not to split the difference but to understand the underlying needs deeply enough to create a solution that addresses them together.

Useful when.

  • Both sets of concerns are important and a simple trade-off would damage the outcome.
  • The issue is complex and different perspectives contain information the other side needs.
  • Long-term commitment, trust or joint ownership matters.
  • The team has enough time and psychological safety for real problem solving.

Agile example. Product needs a regulatory feature quickly while Engineering is concerned about reliability. Collaboration explores a thinner compliant slice plus technical safeguards instead of forcing either “ship now” or “delay everything”.

Risk of overuse. Trying to collaborate on every small disagreement can be slow and exhausting.

3. CompromisingMODERATE ASSERTIVENESS · MODERATE COOPERATIVENESS

What it means. Both sides accept a partial solution. Each gets some of what matters, but neither side's concern is fully satisfied.

Useful when.

  • The issue matters to both sides but does not justify the time needed for deep collaboration.
  • Parties have relatively balanced power and mutually exclusive preferences.
  • A temporary settlement is needed while a more complete solution is developed.
  • Time pressure requires a workable middle ground.

Agile example. Two teams share a specialist for one Sprint and agree a temporary 60/40 allocation while capability is built elsewhere.

Risk of overuse. Teams can become too quick to “split the difference” before understanding whether a better integrative option exists.

4. AvoidingUNASSERTIVE · UNCOOPERATIVE

What it means. You do not immediately pursue either side's concern. You may postpone the conversation, step away, gather information or decide that the issue does not deserve attention now.

Useful when.

  • The issue is minor compared with other priorities.
  • Emotions are too high for productive dialogue and a cooling-off period is needed.
  • You need more information before engaging.
  • Someone else is better positioned to resolve the issue.

Agile example. A heated retrospective discussion is paused for 20 minutes so people can regulate, collect facts and return with a clearer conversation.

Risk of overuse. Important conflicts can remain unresolved, accumulate resentment or reappear later in more damaging form.

5. AccommodatingUNASSERTIVE · COOPERATIVE

What it means. You deliberately give priority to the other person's concern over your own in this situation.

Useful when.

  • You discover that you are wrong or the other person's evidence is stronger.
  • The issue matters much more to the other person than it does to you.
  • Preserving goodwill is more valuable than winning a low-stakes point.
  • You want to support learning or allow someone else to try their approach when the risk is acceptable.

Agile example. A developer prefers one internal implementation approach but accommodates another engineer's preference because both are safe and the issue is low consequence.

Risk of overuse. Your needs can disappear from the system, creating frustration, weak boundaries or hidden disagreement.

How do you choose a mode?

Instead of asking “What is my conflict style?”, ask “What does this situation require?” Consider the attributes below before choosing or changing modes.

Importance

How important is the issue to you? How important is it to the other person?

Time

Do you have time for deeper discussion, or is immediate action necessary?

Complexity

Is this a simple trade-off or a complex problem that needs multiple perspectives?

Trust

Can both sides openly discuss real concerns, or is psychological safety currently low?

Stress

Is the conflict occurring under pressure that reduces listening and reasoning quality?

Relationship

How important is the ongoing working relationship and future cooperation?

Important: You can change modes as the situation changes. A conflict might begin with Competing during an emergency, move to Avoiding briefly to cool down, and later move into Collaborating for the long-term solution.

How an Agile Coach can use the model

  1. Describe the conflict situation without labelling the people.
  2. Ask each person which concern they are trying to protect.
  3. Explore the relative importance of the issue to both sides.
  4. Identify the mode currently being used from observable behaviour.
  5. Ask whether that mode fits the situation or is simply habitual.
  6. Choose a different mode deliberately if the current one is not helping.
  7. Review afterwards: what happened, what did the mode enable, and what did it cost?

Useful coaching questions

“How important is this outcome to you, and how important do you think it is to the other person?”

“Are you trying to solve, win, preserve the relationship, buy time, or let something go?”

“What would collaboration require that is not available right now?”

“What could happen if you continue using this same mode?”

Common mistakes

“Collaborating is always best.”

No. The model explicitly treats all five modes as potentially useful. Context determines fit.

“This is my personality.”

TKI modes describe conflict-handling behaviour and preferences, not fixed personality identities.

“Avoiding is weak.”

Strategic postponement can be wise when the issue is trivial, information is missing or emotions are too high.

“Competing is always aggressive.”

Decisiveness can be appropriate when safety, urgency or a non-negotiable boundary requires it.
