Decision Rights Map

A lightweight way to make recurring decisions explicit: who decides, who advises, who provides evidence, and who only needs visibility. The goal is fewer approval queues and less accountability without authority.

Core idea: Role ambiguity often shows up as decision latency. Rather than writing larger role descriptions, map the real decisions that repeatedly wait, bounce, escalate or get revisited.
Use this when people say: “I thought they owned that”, “we still need approval”, “the PO is accountable but cannot decide”, or “everyone needs to sign off”.

The four columns

1 · DECISION

What is being decided?

Write the actual decision, not the meeting or task. Example: “Which onboarding rule enters the next release?”

2 · DECIDER

Who is accountable for the final decision?

Use one clear accountable decision owner wherever possible.

3 · ADVISERS / EVIDENCE

Whose input is needed?

Separate valuable advice, expertise and evidence from formal approval.

4 · VISIBILITY

Who needs to know?

Some stakeholders need transparency, not veto power.

How to facilitate it

  1. Collect the last 5–10 decisions that were delayed, escalated, revisited or politically difficult.
  2. Rewrite each as a clear decision statement.
  3. Name the person/role that actually made the final call—not the person who organised the meeting.
  4. List advisers and evidence providers separately.
  5. Mark every approval that is legally, regulatorily or policy-required.
  6. Challenge every remaining “approval”: is it genuinely required, or simply inherited habit?
  7. Agree a simpler decision path and test it for a defined period.

Coaching insight: Advising is not the same as approving. Many slow organisations accidentally convert every expert opinion into a permission gate.

Powerful coaching questions

“What decision is actually waiting here?”

“Who can say yes—not just who can say no?”

“Which approval is mandatory, and which one exists because we have always done it?”

“What evidence would allow this decision to move closer to the team?”

“Who needs consultation versus permission?”

“Where does accountability exist without authority?”

Watch for

Turning it into RACI bureaucracy

Map only consequential recurring decisions; do not catalogue every small action.

Ignoring real governance

Some decisions genuinely require specific authorities. Clarify and design around them rather than pretending they do not exist.

Multiple final deciders

Consensus can be useful, but unclear final accountability creates waiting when agreement fails.

Moving authority without evidence

Autonomy works best with clear boundaries, competence, transparency and feedback.

Agile Orbit technique: Decision Rights Map combines decision-flow coaching principles into a lightweight practical canvas. Use it to improve clarity and flow, not to create another governance artifact.
