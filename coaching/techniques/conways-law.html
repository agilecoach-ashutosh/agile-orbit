Conway's Law

System designs tend to mirror the communication structures of the organisations that create them.

Core idea: Coach architecture and team design together when every change requires repeated cross-team coordination.

Understand the method first

Communication structure

Who needs to coordinate with whom to make a change?

System structure

Which components, services and interfaces require coordinated change?

Reinforcing loop

Organisation and architecture can reinforce each other, making the same dependency patterns persist.

Lineage: Melvin Conway articulated the observation in 1967/1968. It is widely known as Conway’s Law and is frequently applied to software architecture and organisation design.

How to use it

  1. Trace a representative change from idea to production.
  2. Mark every team, component and approval that must coordinate.
  3. Compare organisational boundaries with technical boundaries.
  4. Identify high-frequency coupling that repeatedly slows delivery.
  5. Experiment with clearer ownership, interfaces or team interaction patterns where appropriate.

Coach with it

Use the method as a lens for inquiry, not as a rigid prescription. Useful coaching questions include:

“Why does this change require these teams to coordinate?”

“Does the architecture reflect an old organisation design?”

“What boundary would allow one team to test and deploy independently?”

“Would changing team boundaries without architecture changes actually help?”

Agile example

Every feature in a portfolio product requires three teams because a shared component has split ownership. The coach helps leaders see the architecture/team-design loop rather than blaming teams for “poor collaboration.”

Watch for

Watch for

Conway’s Law is an observation and lens, not a deterministic formula.

Watch for

Reorganisation alone does not magically decouple a tightly coupled system.
