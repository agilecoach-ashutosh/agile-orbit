Interest-Based Reframing

Move a conflict from competing positions—“my solution versus yours”—toward the underlying needs, risks, constraints and outcomes each side is trying to protect.

← Back to Conflict CoachingHow to use it ↓
Core idea: A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why it matters. When the coach helps both sides uncover the interests underneath positions, new options often become possible.
POSITION

“We need two weeks.”

A proposed answer or demand.

UNDERLYING INTEREST

“We need enough time to protect reliability.”

The concern the position is trying to protect.

How to use it

  1. Ask each side to state their current position without debate.
  2. Ask what important need, risk, outcome or constraint sits underneath that position.
  3. Keep asking until the answer moves from a proposed solution toward what matters.
  4. Reflect both sets of interests in neutral language.
  5. Reframe the conflict as a shared design problem that must protect both important interests.
  6. Generate multiple options before choosing one.
  7. Evaluate options against the interests and real constraints, not against who proposed them.

Worked Agile example

Position A — Product: “This must go live Friday.”

Position B — Engineering: “We need another two weeks.”

Product interest

Evidence is needed before a regulatory milestone; missing it creates business and compliance risk.

Engineering interest

The current implementation has reliability uncertainty and insufficient test evidence for a full release.

Reframed problem: “How might we produce credible regulatory evidence by Friday while keeping production reliability risk within an acceptable boundary?”

That reframing creates options that the original positions hid: a thinner compliant slice, limited rollout, feature flag, controlled environment, additional automated evidence, or a staged release.

Useful coaching questions

“What are you trying to protect with that position?”

“If your preferred solution disappeared, what outcome would still matter?”

“What risk are you unwilling to accept?”

“What would a good outcome need to preserve for both sides?”

“Which constraint is real, and which part is our current solution?”

“What option have we not considered because we started by arguing solutions?”

When this technique works well

  • Two sides are arguing different solutions to the same underlying problem.
  • The relationship needs to continue after the decision.
  • Both sides have legitimate concerns that cannot simply be ignored.
  • The conflict is still low enough that people can listen and reason together.
Not sufficient for severe conflict: When a team has escalated into entrenched hostility, use de-escalation, mediation or stronger organisational support before trying a normal interest-based conversation.

Watch for

Fake interests

“My interest is that you use my solution” is still a position. Ask what makes that solution important.

Ignoring power

Good reframing cannot erase legitimate authority, regulation or decision rights. Make those constraints explicit.

Endless exploration

Once interests are clear, the group still needs a decision rule and a timely decision.

Forcing win-win

Some trade-offs are real. Reframing improves the quality of the choice; it does not guarantee everyone gets everything.

Technique lineage: Interest-based negotiation and mediation approaches separate stated positions from the underlying interests they are intended to protect. Agile Orbit applies that principle to team and product conflict.
