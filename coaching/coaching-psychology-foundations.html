Coaching Theories

Theories help explain why coaching can create learning, awareness and change. This page follows the seven theories identified in the supplied Coaching Theories handout.

Why theory matters: the handout connects coaching with learning and notes outcomes such as greater self-awareness, self-acceptance, confidence, broader perspectives, improved goal attainment, communication and problem solving. Its purpose is to explore theories that inform coaching and enable new learning.

01 · Humanistic Psychology

Growth, autonomy and the person-centred relationship

Core idea: people have an innate capacity for growth, self-awareness and self-direction.

The handout presents Carl Rogers as a pioneer of this perspective. His person-centred approach challenged the assumption that the helping professional necessarily “knows best” and questioned heavy reliance on advice, persuasion, teaching, diagnosis and interpretation.

Its humanistic view is illustrated with an acorn metaphor: when the conditions are sufficiently nurturing, the acorn does not need to be instructed how to become a tree. Its potential can move toward actualisation. In the same way, the handout describes people as essentially trustworthy and capable of greater self-understanding and self-direction when the relationship supports that growth.

What this means for coaching

Respect autonomy. Create conditions where the client can understand themselves, make meaning and generate their own direction rather than treating the coach as the authority on the client's life.

Techniques named in the handout

Active listening, unconditional positive regard and values-driven questions.

Example from the handout

When a coachee feels lost, the coach asks: “What truly matters to you?” to help reconnect the client with values.

02 · Cognitive Behavioural

Thinking mediates emotion and action

Core idea: thoughts influence feelings and actions. Reframing limiting beliefs can lead to different outcomes.

The handout uses a simple sequence to show that an event and our response are not necessarily the same thing. What we think about an event influences what we feel and what we do next.

1 · EventI see a tiger
2 · ThoughtI think I'm in danger
3 · FeelingI feel afraid
4 · ActionI run

The practical coaching implication in the handout is to examine the interpretation between the event and the response. This creates room to test beliefs, notice distorted thinking and consider alternative interpretations rather than assuming the first thought is simply reality.

Techniques named in the handout

ABC model, challenging distorted thinking and cognitive restructuring.

Useful coaching stance

Explore the evidence behind a belief instead of immediately reassuring, contradicting or replacing it for the client.

Example from the handout

A coachee fears speaking up. The coach asks: “What evidence supports that fear?”

03 · Adult Learning Theory

Adults learn through ownership, relevance and experience

Core idea: Adult Learning Theory, also called Andragogy in the handout and associated with Malcolm Knowles, emphasises that adults are self-directed, bring experience into learning, and are motivated by real-life challenges and goals.

Self-direction

Adults prefer to take responsibility for their learning.

Experience-based

Learning builds on the learner's previous experiences.

Goal-oriented

Clear and relevant goals increase motivation to learn.

Relevance

Learning needs a connection to real-life situations.

Problem-centred

Adults learn through real-world problems rather than only abstract content.

Internal motivation

Personal growth can be a stronger driver than external rewards.

How the handout applies this to coaching

Client Ownership

The coach acts as a facilitator rather than instructor, allowing clients to set goals and reflect on their own insights.

Experience as a Resource

The client's lived experience becomes material for learning and change rather than something to bypass.

Examples from the handout

Instead of telling a leader how to improve delegation: “What have you tried so far, and what patterns do you notice?”

To draw on past experience: “What past success can you draw on in this current challenge?”

04 · Solution Focused

Build from resources, strengths and the preferred future

Core idea: Solution-Focused Theory, described in the handout as originating in therapy with Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg, emphasises solutions rather than extended problem analysis. It assumes clients already possess resources, strengths and knowledge that can support positive change.

Rather than spending most of the conversation analysing root causes, the handout points to questions such as:

  • What is working?
  • What would be different if things got better?
  • What small step could move you closer to that?

Goal-Centred Coaching

Help the client define what success would look like.

Amplifying Strengths

Explore times when the client was successful and what could be repeated or adapted.

Scaling Questions

Use a scale such as 1–10 to notice progress, motivation and small next steps.

Example from the handout

A team leader feels overwhelmed. The coach asks about a recent moment when things went well. The client identifies clearer delegation, advance preparation and greater trust in the team. The coach then asks how that can be repeated this week.

06 · Positive Psychology

Strengths, well-being and optimal functioning

Core idea: Positive Psychology focuses on the scientific study of human strengths, well-being and optimal functioning.

The handout associates this perspective with Martin Seligman and describes a shift from focusing only on what is wrong toward also exploring what is working, meaningful and capable of being strengthened. It highlights the PERMA model:

PPositive Emotion
EEngagement
RRelationships
MMeaning
AAccomplishment

The handout links this theory with practices intended to enhance well-being, resilience and the use of strengths.

Techniques named

VIA Character Strengths and Gallup Strengths.

Coaching orientation

Explore what the client already does well and how existing strengths may be used more deliberately.

Example from the handout

A client feels stuck. The coach draws attention to strengths such as creativity and curiosity and asks how those strengths could be used at work.

07 · Systems Theory

Look beyond the individual to the whole system

Core idea: the characteristics of individual parts do not by themselves explain the whole; what emerges from their interaction creates systems that can be complex and unpredictable.

The handout describes organisations as living systems in which people continually interact with their environments. Stability and equilibrium are therefore temporary rather than permanent states. It uses clouds as an analogy for self-organisation: their form changes as surrounding conditions and patterns change.

The handout also emphasises that transformation involves navigating tensions such as openness and closure, and that coaches benefit from understanding how a client's frame of reference interacts with wider complexity, culture and polarities.

Shift the unit of attention

Instead of asking only “What is wrong with this person?”, examine relationships, expectations, culture, environment and feedback loops around them.

Notice temporary stability

What appears stable today may be the current expression of a changing system rather than a permanent condition.

Example from the handout

A senior leader blames themselves for weak team performance and low morale. The coach broadens the lens by mapping stakeholders, expectations and unspoken norms. Pressure for speed and a culture where the team does not feel safe to push back become visible, shifting the conversation from only the leader's style toward the wider system.
