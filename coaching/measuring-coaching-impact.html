Measuring Coaching Impact

Coaching is an investment. The learning material looks at impact at individual, team and organisational levels and recommends using several forms of evidence rather than one number alone.

Kirkpatrick's model as an evaluation lens

Level 1Reaction

Coach–coachee feedback and satisfaction surveys.

Level 2Learning

Awareness, clarity and mindset shifts.

Level 3Application / Behaviour

Change in leadership behaviours and evidence such as 360-degree feedback.

Level 4Results

Business or organisational results such as engagement, productivity, retention, performance uplift or cost-benefit measures.

Leedham Pyramid Model — Coaching Scorecard

Business benefits
Results
Outer personal benefits
Skills and behaviour
Inner personal benefits
Clarity and focus, confidence and motivation
Foundation factors
Coaching process, coach attributes, coach skills and coaching environment

Tools to measure impact

Before–After self-assessments

Compare the client's own view before and after an engagement.

360-degree feedback

Use observations from people who work with the client.

Behavioural observation

Look for visible changes in behaviour, not only stated intention.

Pulse surveys

Use lightweight recurring feedback where appropriate.

Manager & stakeholder interviews

Capture changes noticed by people around the client.

Coaching logs & journals

Track learning, commitments and reflections over time.

Daily Conscious Behaviours

The learning map also lists a daily conscious-behaviours monitoring matrix as an impact tool.

Impact variables to consider

The learning map highlights that measured outcomes can be influenced by more than coaching alone. Consider the context around the engagement:

  • length of the coaching engagement;
  • reason or reasons for coaching;
  • previous experience of the coachee, manager and/or stakeholders with coaching and other forms of development;
  • experience level of the coach;
  • method used to match coach and coachee;
  • real-world financial performance metrics such as revenue, savings or profit changes under the person's area of responsibility;
  • new products or services developed during the period;
  • time to market;
  • retention and turnover, including comparisons where a useful matched comparison exists;
  • promotion outcomes, including comparisons where appropriate.

Measurement principle

Use the evidence to understand change and value. Keep context visible when interpreting results, especially when business outcomes have many possible causes.

Frameworks: the source learning material applies Kirkpatrick's evaluation model to coaching and references Leedham's (2005) coaching scorecard/pyramid. The measurement tools and impact variables above are drawn from the supplied Coggle and session deck.
