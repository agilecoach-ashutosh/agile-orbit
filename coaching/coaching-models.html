Frameworks that support—not control—the conversation

A coaching model is a map, not a script. It can help the coach orient, notice what may be missing and give a conversation shape. But the client is still the terrain. When following the acronym becomes more important than listening, the model has stopped helping.

Use models lightly. Good coaching rarely moves neatly from box 1 to box 2. A client may discover a new goal while exploring reality, return to an earlier assumption after discussing options, or decide that awareness—not action—is the useful outcome for today.

Choose by the client's need, not your favourite acronym

These are practical orientations rather than rigid rules. The same conversation may borrow from more than one model.

01 · CORE MODELGoal-focused

GROW

Goal → Reality → Options → Will / Way Forward

A simple structure for moving from what the client wants, through what is happening now, toward possibilities and chosen action.

What each stage is doing

  • Goal: establish what the client wants from the conversation or situation.
  • Reality: explore what is happening now without rushing into fixing.
  • Options: widen possibilities before narrowing to one answer.
  • Will: convert insight into a choice, commitment or next experiment.

Questions that keep ownership with the client

What would make this conversation valuable for you?
What is happening now—and what part of that matters most?
What else could you try if you did not have to decide yet?
What do you want to do, and how committed are you to that?
Watch for: turning GROW into an interrogation. Reality may reveal a different goal; options may create new awareness. Move with the client rather than forcing the sequence.
Performance Consultants identifies GROW as Goals, Reality, Options and Will and describes it as a flexible coaching and problem-solving framework. External reference ↗
02 · GROW VARIANTTopic emerging

TGROW

Topic → Goal → Reality → Options → Will

TGROW adds an explicit Topic stage before GROW. It is especially useful when the client arrives with several issues, a broad concern or uncertainty about what deserves attention.

The extra value of “Topic”

Topic is not simply “What is the problem?” It gives the client room to locate the real conversation. The presenting issue may be workload, but the useful topic may become boundaries, identity, confidence or a difficult relationship.

Questions to establish the topic

What feels most worth exploring today?
There are a few threads here—which one has the most energy for you?
If we could make progress on only one part today, which part would matter?
What would you like to be different by the end of this conversation?
Watch for: choosing the topic for the client because one issue sounds more important to you. Topic-setting is already part of the coaching work.
TGROW is commonly used as a GROW variation that begins with Topic before Goal, Reality, Options and Will. External reference ↗
03 · DEVELOPMENTALRelationship & depth

CLEAR

Contract → Listen → Explore → Action → Review

A model associated with Peter Hawkins that places explicit emphasis on the coaching agreement, deep listening, exploration and learning from the session itself.

What each stage is doing

  • Contract: agree purpose, desired outcome, boundaries and how you will work.
  • Listen: receive the client's story, meaning, emotion and patterns without prematurely steering.
  • Explore: deepen awareness, assumptions, perspectives, tensions and possibilities.
  • Action: identify what the client wants to take forward.
  • Review: notice what shifted, what was learned and what may need continued attention.

Useful questions

What would you like from me in this conversation?
What am I not yet hearing that feels important?
What assumption might be shaping how you see this?
What changed for you while we were talking?
Watch for: treating “Action” as mandatory. In a developmental conversation, a meaningful shift in awareness may be the most useful outcome before action becomes clear.
The CLEAR model is attributed to Peter Hawkins and is commonly described as Contracting, Listening, Exploring, Action and Review. External reference ↗
04 · SOLUTION-FOCUSEDResources & progress

OSKAR

Outcome → Scaling → Know-how & Resources → Affirm & Action → Review

Developed by Mark McKergow and Paul Z. Jackson, OSKAR brings solution-focused principles into a practical coaching structure. It deliberately looks for progress, capability and what is already working.

What each stage is doing

  • Outcome: describe the preferred future rather than only the problem to remove.
  • Scaling: make progress visible and explore what is already present at the current number.
  • Know-how & Resources: surface skills, relationships, exceptions and previous successes.
  • Affirm & Action: recognise useful capability and identify the next small move.
  • Review: notice what has improved and what should continue.

Questions that uncover capability

Suppose this were working well—what would you notice?
On a 0–10 scale, where are you now? What makes it that number rather than lower?
When has even a small part of the outcome happened already?
What would one point higher look like in observable terms?
Watch for: using “positive” questions to bypass genuine difficulty. Solution focus is not denial; the client's experience still needs to be heard.
SFWork states that OSKAR was developed by Mark McKergow and Paul Z. Jackson around 2000 and defines the stages used here. External reference ↗
05 · APPROACHPreferred future

Solution-Focused Coaching

Preferred future → Exceptions → Resources → Progress → Small steps

Solution-Focused Coaching is less a single fixed acronym and more a way of directing attention: toward desired outcomes, existing competence, exceptions to the problem and achievable progress.

Core moves

  • Describe what the client wants instead of only what they want to stop.
  • Find exceptions: times the difficulty was absent, smaller or handled differently.
  • Identify skills, support and conditions already contributing to progress.
  • Use scaling to make small differences visible.
  • Design the next useful experiment rather than a perfect master plan.

Questions that change the direction of attention

If this were better, what would be happening instead?
When is this problem less powerful? What is different then?
What are you already doing that you should not accidentally stop doing?
What small sign would tell you that things are moving?
Watch for: assuming that every client wants to move away from problem exploration immediately. Sometimes understanding and naming the experience is itself important before a preferred future can be useful.
The Handbook of Coaching Psychology describes Solution-Focused Coaching as outcome-oriented and competence-based, giving central attention to the client's strengths, knowledge and experience. External reference ↗
06 · STRENGTHS-BASEDPossibility & change

Appreciative Inquiry

Discover → Dream → Design → Destiny / Deliver

Appreciative Inquiry (AI) explores the “positive core”: strengths, capabilities, relationships and moments when a person or system has been at its best, then uses that learning to imagine and create what could be next.

The 4-D cycle

  • Discover: identify and appreciate what works and when the system is at its best.
  • Dream: imagine what might be possible if those strengths were amplified.
  • Design: shape the conditions, behaviours or structures that could support that future.
  • Destiny / Deliver: act, experiment, learn and sustain movement.

Questions for appreciative exploration

Tell me about a time when this was working unusually well. What made that possible?
What strengths were you using at your best?
If those conditions became more normal, what might become possible?
What could you deliberately create or repeat now?
Watch for: confusing appreciation with forced positivity. AI can acknowledge difficult reality while choosing to inquire into strengths, possibility and generative change.
The Cooperrider Center and Positive Change describe the classic 4-D cycle as Discovery, Dream, Design and Destiny/Deliver. External reference ↗
THE MODEL BEHIND THE MODELS

Listen first. Orient second.

The most transferable coaching skill is not remembering six frameworks. It is noticing what the client needs next. Models are useful when they increase presence and choice; they become limiting when they replace curiosity.

Stay client-ledThe client's agenda, language, pace and meaning should remain more important than completing a sequence.
Move non-linearlyReturn, loop, pause or abandon a stage when new awareness changes the conversation.
Contract visiblyWhen changing direction or introducing a tool, make the shift transparent rather than silently steering.
Do not manufacture actionA session can create value through insight, clarity, acceptance or a changed perspective—not only a task list.
Watch your preferenceIf you always reach for the same model, ask whether it serves the client or simply makes you feel competent.
Use the lightest structureStart with presence and inquiry. Add structure only when it helps the client think more clearly.
