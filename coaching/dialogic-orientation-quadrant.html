Dialogic Orientation Quadrant (DOQ)

Haesun Moon's Dialogic Orientation Quadrant is a research-informed heuristic for noticing how coaching dialogue is jointly shaped, moment by moment. Rather than treating questions as neutral containers, DOQ helps a coach notice the direction their language invites—and become more deliberate about that choice.

WHY THIS MATTERS

Your next response selects a direction

Institute of Coaching material describes DOQ as mapping conversation across two dimensions: time (past ↔ future) and preference (unwanted ↔ wanted). That creates four possible conversational orientations. The point is not to label one quadrant “good” and another “bad”; it is to notice where the dialogue is going and whether that direction serves the client's purpose.

Interactional lens: what matters is not only what the coach intended to ask, but what the exchange does next. The client's answer gives evidence about the conversational direction that was co-created.

A practical way to see the four orientations

The grid below is an Agile Orbit practice representation of the publicly described DOQ dimensions—not a reproduction of Haesun Moon's proprietary training materials or official diagrams.

TIME · Past ↔ Future    |    PREFERENCE · Unwanted ↔ Wanted
Past + Unwanted

What has been difficult?

Conversation can orient toward problems, disappointments, constraints, breakdowns or what has not worked.

Example: “When has this felt most difficult?”
Past + Wanted

What evidence already exists?

Conversation can orient toward resources, exceptions, previous progress, strengths or moments when the preferred pattern was already present.

Example: “When have you already handled something like this well?”
Future + Unwanted

What do you want to prevent or leave behind?

Conversation can orient toward feared continuation, risks, consequences or what the client does not want to keep experiencing.

Example: “What are you most concerned will continue if nothing changes?”
Future + Wanted

What would preferred change look like?

Conversation can orient toward purpose, possibility, preferred outcomes and signs that movement is happening.

Example: “What would tell you this is moving in the direction you want?”
These labels and example prompts are Agile Orbit's explanatory language for practice. They are not presented as official DOQ quadrant names.

Four disciplines for using the lens

Notice before steeringFirst recognise where the client is already orienting. Do not jump to a preferred-future question merely because it sounds more “coaching-like.”
Follow purposeAsk whether the direction supports what the client wants from the conversation rather than what the coach prefers to explore.
Watch the responseThe client's next words show what your question made more discussable. Treat that response as data about the interaction.
Choose deliberatelyYou can stay, deepen, contrast or shift orientation—but do it consciously rather than by habitual question pattern.
ONE SENTENCE · FOUR DIRECTIONS
Client: “I keep getting ignored in meetings.”
PAST + UNWANTED“When has being ignored felt most frustrating?”Invites more detail about the difficult pattern.
PAST + WANTED“When have people listened to you more closely?”Invites evidence of exceptions, resources or different conditions.
FUTURE + UNWANTED“What are you worried will keep happening if this continues?”Invites exploration of feared consequences.
FUTURE + WANTED“What would being heard look like in your next meeting?”Invites description of preferred change.

None of these questions is automatically the “right” question. Their value depends on timing, the client's purpose, what has already been said and what becomes useful next.

Use DOQ as a review tool

Its strongest value may be after the conversation, when you can study a transcript or recording without relying on memory.

Review your transcript

  • What client words did I select and respond to?
  • Which time orientation did my response invite?
  • Did my wording orient toward wanted or unwanted experience?
  • What did the client say immediately after my question?
  • Where did the dialogue gain energy or become narrower?

Try an alternative response

  • What else could I have responded to in the same sentence?
  • What different orientation would that have invited?
  • How might I preserve more of the client's own wording?
  • Where did I introduce my interpretation?
  • What would I experiment with in the next session?

Coach watch-outs

Do not turn it into a scriptThe framework is a noticing lens, not a sequence of quadrants the client must visit.
Do not force “positive” talkWanted-future conversation is not always the next useful move. Difficulty, loss and risk may need genuine attention.
Do not analyse only the coachDOQ is interactional. Look at what coach and client build together, not just whether the coach asked a technically elegant question.
Do not ignore timingThe same wording can land differently depending on what came immediately before it.
Do not detach language from purposeA conversational shift is useful only if it serves the client's chosen direction.
Do not confuse mapping with judgementLocating an exchange is about awareness and choice, not scoring the coach or declaring a quadrant wrong.
PRACTICE EXERCISE

Pause on one line of transcript

Choose a client sentence with several possible threads. Write four different responses, each intentionally orienting the conversation differently across time and preference. Then ask yourself: Which response most closely serves the client's stated purpose right now? This develops choice—not formula.
