Move from habitual confirmation toward data, empathy and emerging possibility.

Widen attention from your own internal noise to the client's words, context, unsaid signals and meaning.

Lens 1

Theory U — four levels of listening

The four levels describe a shift in the quality and orientation of attention.

1

Downloading

Past · Habitual

Judgement, filtering and reconfirming old opinions. New information is interpreted through what is already believed.

Learning map result: No change
2

Factual

Present · Data

Open mind. Notice new or disconfirming information rather than only evidence that confirms an existing view.

Learning map result: Learning & improvement
3

Empathic

Person · Emotion

Open heart. Attend to the person's experience and emotion, allowing connection and trust to deepen.

Learning map result: Connection & trust
4

Generative

Future · Potential

Open will and presence. Listen for what may be emerging rather than only what already exists.

Learning map result: Innovation & co-creation
Useful self-check: Am I genuinely hearing new information, or mainly collecting evidence for the story I already have?
Lens 2

Deep Listening — five levels of attention

According to the Deep Listening material, effective listening widens attention beyond the literal words being spoken.

Listening to self

Notice inside: internal distractions, assumptions, reactions and the urge to prepare your response.

Listening to content

Listen to the words: the actual information, language and phrases the client is using.

Listening for context

Widen the frame: situation, environment, timing and surrounding conditions shaping what is being said.

Listening for what's unsaid

Notice beyond words: emotion, pauses, shifts in energy, body language and what may not yet be expressed directly.

Listening for meaning

Listen underneath: the need, intention or deeper meaning the client may be making through the conversation.

The goal is not to “decode” the client.

What you notice becomes material for curiosity and exploration, not a conclusion about what the client secretly means.

Why attention drifts

The listening attention gap

Your supplied material uses approximate speaking, listening and thinking speeds to explain why the mind can race ahead during a conversation.

Speaking125–150words per minute

The pace at which spoken words are typically being delivered in the source material.

Listening400–500words per minute

The source describes listening capacity as faster than the rate at which people usually speak.

Thinking / processing900–1,200words per minute

With more processing capacity available, the mind can race ahead while another person is still speaking.

Approximate figures shown as presented in the supplied Deep Listening material.
DriftAttention wanders away from what the client is saying.
JudgeThe mind filters or interprets before the client has completed the thought.
PrepareThe coach starts composing the next question instead of continuing to listen.
Apply it

Use the spare attention differently

Deep listening asks the coach to use attention for presence rather than for racing ahead.

Step 1 · Notice

Catch yourself

Notice internal distraction, interpretation or the urge to formulate the next question.

Step 2 · Return

Come back to the client

Return attention to words, context, emotion, pauses and what appears important to the client.

Step 3 · Choose

Respond after listening

Allow silence if useful. Ask or reflect from what emerged rather than from a pre-planned question.

Coaching implication: the gap between speaking and mental processing makes it easy to become distracted or prepare a response too early. Deep listening therefore emphasises presence, silence and focused attention.
Reflect on practice

Six questions for the coach

Use these after a session, during supervision, or as a short preparation check before coaching.

01What am I noticing in the client's words?
02What context is shaping what is being said?
03What may be communicated without being stated directly?
04What meaning is the client making?
05Am I hearing new information—or mainly confirming what I already think?
06While the client is speaking, where does my unused attention go?
