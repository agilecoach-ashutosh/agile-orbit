Listen beyond the words

The learning map uses two complementary listening lenses: Theory U asks where the listening is coming from; the Deep Listening framework asks what the listener is attending to.

Theory U — four levels of listening

Downloading

Orientation: Past / habitual.

Pattern: Judgement, filtering and reconfirming old opinions.

Result noted in the learning map: no change.

Factual

Orientation: Present / data.

Pattern: Open mind; noticing disconfirming or new data.

Result noted in the learning map: learning & improvement.

Empathic

Orientation: Person / emotion.

Pattern: Open heart.

Result noted in the learning map: connection & trust.

Generative

Orientation: Future / potential.

Pattern: Open will & presence.

Result noted in the learning map: innovation & co-creation.

Deep Listening — five levels of attention

According to Oscar Trimboli / the Deep Listening Institute, listening happens across five levels. The levels encourage the listener to widen attention beyond the literal words being spoken.

1. Listening to selfNotice your internal distractions, assumptions and reactions while the other person is speaking.
2. Listening to contentAttend to the actual words being spoken and the information the speaker is sharing.
3. Listening for contextNotice the situation, environment, timing and surrounding conditions that shape what is being said.
4. Listening for what’s unsaidAttend to emotions, pauses, changes in energy, body language and what may not yet have been expressed directly.
5. Listening for meaningListen for the deeper need, intention or meaning behind the words.

Why deep listening is difficult

Your source material highlights a gap between speaking speed and the amount of mental processing available while listening. That spare capacity can make it easy for the listener to drift, judge, rehearse a response or jump ahead before the speaker has finished.

ActivitySpeed per minute (approx.)
Speaking125–150 words per minute
Listening400–500 words per minute
Thinking / brain processing900–1,200 words per minute
Speaking is relatively slow. We typically speak at around 125–150 words per minute.
Listening occurs faster. The source material describes the brain as being able to absorb spoken words more efficiently than we can speak them.
Thinking is fastest. With much more processing capacity available, the mind can race ahead during a conversation.
Coaching implication: this gap makes it easy to become distracted or start formulating the next response before the client has finished. Deep listening therefore asks for presence, silence and focused attention rather than using the spare mental capacity to prepare the next question.

Listening reflection

  • What am I noticing in the client's words?
  • What context is shaping what is being said?
  • What may be communicated without being stated directly?
  • What meaning is the client making?
  • Am I listening for new information—or mainly confirming what I already think?
  • While the client is speaking, where does my unused attention go?
