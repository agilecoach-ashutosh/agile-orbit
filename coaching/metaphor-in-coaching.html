Metaphor in Coaching

When a client says “I am on a treadmill,” “I have hit a wall,” or “everything feels foggy,” they have already created a rich representation of their experience. The coach's job is not to decode it for them. The opportunity is to become curious about what the client's own image contains.

CORE PRACTICE

Stay with the client's image before translating it

Metaphor can make an experience more vivid and explorable. The 2025 ICF competency glossary explicitly recognises metaphor as a way clients can understand experiences, feelings or situations through more vivid concepts. In practice, that does not give the coach permission to decide what the image means. Preserve the client's ownership of the metaphor and explore it with curiosity.

01

Notice

Catch the image, phrase or comparison the client naturally uses. Do not rush past it in search of the next question.

02

Explore

Use the client's exact words and invite description, movement, relationship, possibility or change inside that image.

03

Return ownership

Let the client decide what the metaphor means, whether it remains useful and what—if anything—they want to do with the insight.

Interpretation vs exploration

Client

“I feel like I am running on a treadmill.”

Coach interprets

“So you are exhausted?” Perhaps—but the coach has replaced the client's image with the coach's meaning.

Coach explores

“What is that treadmill like?”
“What keeps it moving?”
“Where are you on it?”
“What happens if it slows down?”
“What would stepping off mean to you?”

Question library for exploring a metaphor

Use these as prompts, not a sequence. One clean question followed by genuine listening is usually more useful than interrogating every part of the image.

Describe the image

  • What is that [client's word] like?
  • What kind of [wall / fog / road / weight] is it?
  • What stands out most in that image?
  • Is there anything else about it?

Position & relationship

  • Where are you in relation to it?
  • What is closest to you?
  • What feels far away?
  • Who or what else is present?

Movement & change

  • What is moving—and what is not?
  • What happens next in the image?
  • If one thing changed, what would you notice first?
  • What would make movement possible?

Choice & meaning

  • What does this image help you see?
  • What becomes possible from here?
  • What part of the metaphor feels most important?
  • What, if anything, do you want to take from it?

Mini practice: stay in their language

“My career has hit a wall.”

Explore

“What kind of wall is it?” “How high does it seem?” “What is on the other side, if anything?” Avoid deciding that the wall automatically represents fear, bureaucracy or lack of confidence.

“I am carrying everyone.”

Explore

“What are you carrying?” “How are you carrying it?” “What happens if you put one thing down?” Notice whether the client changes or develops the image as they speak.

“I am at a crossroads.”

Explore

“What roads can you see?” “What is different about each?” “Do you have to choose now?” “Is there another route you cannot yet see?”

“It feels foggy.”

Explore

“What can you see through the fog?” “How dense is it?” “What would a little more visibility give you?” “Do you need the fog to disappear completely before moving?”

“I am juggling too many balls.”

Explore

“Which ones are you most afraid of dropping?” “Are they all yours to juggle?” “What happens if one is placed down rather than dropped?”

“I am in a cage.”

Explore carefully

“What is the cage like?” “What keeps you there?” “What choice feels available inside it?” Stay especially alert to emotional intensity and do not force the metaphor toward an upbeat resolution.

Coach watch-outs

Do not diagnose the image“The wall means you fear failure” turns exploration into interpretation.
Do not become cleverThe purpose is the client's discovery, not the coach demonstrating creativity with language.
Do not overwork itA metaphor can be useful for one question and then be finished. Follow the client's energy.
Avoid cultural assumptionsThe same image can carry very different meanings across people, contexts and cultures.
Do not force positivityA dark or difficult metaphor does not have to be immediately transformed into a hopeful one.
Use exact client languageSmall substitutions can change meaning. Their “door” is not automatically your “exit.”
WHEN THE COACH OFFERS AN IMAGE

Offer lightly—and let the client reject it

A coach may sometimes notice an image emerging from the conversation. Treat it as a tentative contribution rather than an explanation. Ask permission, make it easy to decline and immediately return meaning-making to the client.

“An image is coming to mind as I listen. Would it be useful if I offered it?”

Then: “What, if anything, fits for you—and what does not?”
