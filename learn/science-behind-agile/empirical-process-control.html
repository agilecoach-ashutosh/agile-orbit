Empirical Process Control

When important outcomes cannot be known reliably in advance, control comes from making reality visible, inspecting it frequently and adapting quickly.

DIRECT SCRUM FOUNDATION

The core idea

In empirical work, knowledge comes from experience and observation rather than assuming an initial model or plan is complete. The 2017 Scrum Guide explicitly described Scrum as founded on empirical process control theory; the current Scrum Guide says Scrum is founded on empiricism and lean thinking.

The loop

Transparency → Inspection → Adaptation
Make the state of work and outcomes visible. Inspect what is actually happening. Change the plan, process or product when the evidence says the current direction is no longer useful.

Why this matters in complex work

When technology, users, dependencies and market conditions interact, prediction has limits. A detailed upfront plan can still be useful, but it should remain a hypothesis rather than a substitute for evidence.

Example

A team believes a new onboarding flow will reduce abandonment. It releases a small usable increment, inspects customer behaviour and support feedback, discovers that the real friction occurs earlier than expected, and changes the Product Backlog accordingly.

What this is not

  • It is not permission to work without goals or planning.
  • It is not frequent meetings without meaningful evidence.
  • It is not inspection without the authority to adapt.
  • It is not short iterations that repeat the same assumptions indefinitely.

Questions to carry into practice

  • What important part of reality is currently hidden?
  • What evidence are we inspecting?
  • How quickly can that evidence change our next decision?
  • Who is actually empowered to adapt when we learn something new?

Source basis: Scrum Guide 2017 and Scrum Guide 2020. The terminology changed, but empiricism, transparency, inspection and adaptation remain central to Scrum theory.
