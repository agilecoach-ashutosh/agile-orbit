SCIENCE · SCRUM · COMPLEXITY

Empiricism and
Process Control Theory

Why short feedback loops matter when reality is too complex to predict completely in advance.

THE STORY

Complex problems rarely stay simple

We are surrounded by complex problems. Even seemingly straightforward problems turn out to be complex on closer inspection. One way to find an answer to these problems is through reasoning or using intuition. You can also rely on previous experiences. But how reliable is experience when you’ve never done something before or when the variables change all the time?

Chemical engineers face the same problem

Chemical engineers have long wrestled with complex challenges too. As it turns out, even seemingly straightforward chemical processes are complex on closer inspection. How do you keep the temperature of a liquid constant? How do you heat crude oil for transport without reducing its quality? So many variables influence these processes that it requires a different approach to control them.

Instead of trying to identify all possible variables and all of their interactions in one comprehensive model, important key variables are constantly monitored with sensors.

When their values exceed certain thresholds, other variables are modified to adjust the system back to the desired state. More heat can be applied, air can be vented, water can be added or removed.

Here, knowledge to drive decisions does not come only from models or assumptions. It comes from a short feedback loop where adjustments are made as needed based on frequent measurements.

01Observe

Measure what is actually happening in the system.

02Compare

Compare the observed state with the desired state or threshold.

03Adjust

Change a controllable variable when the evidence says adjustment is needed.

04Observe again

Inspect the effect of the change and continue the loop.

THE SCRUM CONNECTION

Transparency → Inspection → Adaptation

Complex product development has a similar challenge. Technology, customer behaviour, dependencies, changing needs and new information interact in ways that cannot always be predicted reliably at the start.

Scrum therefore creates frequent opportunities to make the current state visible, inspect what is actually happening and adapt based on what has been learned. The plan is useful, but reality is allowed to change the plan.

A practical product example

A team believes a new onboarding flow will reduce abandonment. Rather than assuming the complete solution is known, it releases a small usable increment, observes customer behaviour and support feedback, and discovers that the real friction occurs earlier than expected. The Product Backlog changes because the evidence changed the team’s understanding.

Why the feedback loop matters

Prediction is not removed. It is kept honest.
Teams still form hypotheses, make plans and use experience. Empiricism adds a disciplined mechanism for allowing observed reality to correct those assumptions before too much time and investment accumulate.

What this is not

  • It is not permission to work without goals or planning.
  • It is not frequent meetings without meaningful evidence.
  • It is not inspection without the authority to adapt.
  • It is not short iterations that repeat the same assumptions indefinitely.

Questions to carry into practice

  • What important part of reality is currently hidden?
  • What are we measuring or observing frequently enough to learn?
  • What threshold or signal would tell us that our current direction needs adjustment?
  • How quickly can new evidence change our next decision?
  • Who is actually empowered to adapt when we learn something new?

Scrum terminology: the 2017 Scrum Guide explicitly described Scrum as founded on empirical process control theory. The 2020 Scrum Guide says Scrum is founded on empiricism and lean thinking. Transparency, inspection and adaptation remain central to Scrum theory.
