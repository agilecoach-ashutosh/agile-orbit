Facilitation Techniques

A visual, practical guide to choosing the right technique for the situation — not just collecting a list of meeting games.

USE THE TOOLBOX

Each technique answers four questions: When? How? What does it look like? and How would this feel in a real team?

CREATE PARTICIPATION1 / 12

1-2-4-All

Use it to: get everyone's thinking into the room before the loudest voice shapes the discussion.

1
Think alone
→
2
Pair
→
4
Small group
→
ALL
Share

When to use

When participation is uneven, the topic is ambiguous, or you want to reduce groupthink before converging.

How to use

Frame one clear question. Give quiet individual reflection first, then pairs, then groups of four, then ask each group to share one important idea.

Real situation

“We keep missing Sprint Goals. Before jumping to solutions, everyone gets a minute to write what they think is really happening.”

CREATE HEALTHY CHALLENGE2 / 12

TRIZ

Use it to: challenge existing habits by first asking how you could make the problem spectacularly worse.

How would we make this REALLY bad?
Overload people
Hide information
Delay every decision

When to use

When a team is stuck in “that’s just how we do it” thinking or needs a safe way to expose harmful patterns.

How to use

Ask for the worst possible practices. Capture them without defending or judging. Then flip each one into its opposite: what would prevent or reverse it?

Real situation

“How could we guarantee our refinement becomes a two-hour status meeting every week?” The answers reveal the behaviours the team needs to stop.

CREATE SHARED EXPECTATIONS3 / 12

Team Agreement

Use it to: make the team's working rules explicit instead of relying on assumptions.

Listen to understand
Challenge ideas, not people
One conversation at a time
Surface concerns early
Be present
Assume positive intent

When to use

At the start of a team relationship, after recurring friction, or when remote/hybrid behaviour needs to be reset.

How to use

Ask “What do we need from each other for this team to work well?” Cluster responses, agree on a short set of observable behaviours, and revisit them.

Real situation

“In retros, the same three people talk. The team agrees on a simple rule: everyone gets thinking time before open discussion.”

BUILD CONSENSUS4 / 12

Roman Voting

Use it to: quickly sense where the group stands when several people have already explored the options.

1
Strongly against
2
Concerns
3
Neutral
4
Support
5
Strong support

When to use

When you want a fast read of the room without turning the decision into a long debate.

How to use

State the proposal clearly. Give everyone a private or visible rating on the agreed scale. Explore the spread, especially the low scores, then re-check.

Real situation

“Can we move our release gate to automated checks?” Most people show support; two show low confidence, revealing exactly where the discussion belongs.

MAKE SENSE OF MANY IDEAS5 / 12

Affinity Mapping

Use it to: turn a large pile of ideas into themes the group can understand and act on.

Theme A
Slow test environment
Build queue
Theme B
Unclear ownership
Late decisions
Theme C
Production defects
Manual checks

When to use

After brainstorming, retrospective data collection, interview notes, or any moment when you have more ideas than you can discuss individually.

How to use

Write one idea per card. Ask people to group similar ideas, name the clusters, then discuss the themes rather than every individual card.

Real situation

“We have 47 retro notes.” Instead of debating all 47, the team groups them into five themes and spots a recurring dependency problem.

CHOOSE A FEW6 / 12

Dot Voting

Use it to: narrow a broad option set to the few items worth deeper discussion.

Improve test automation
Reduce handoffs
Redesign dashboard

When to use

When you have many reasonable choices and need to select a manageable shortlist.

How to use

Give each person a fixed number of votes. Let them place votes on the options they believe matter most, then take the highest-ranked items forward.

Real situation

“We found nine possible improvement actions. Each person gets three votes; the team commits to discussing the top three rather than arguing about all nine.”

DECIDE ON ONE PROPOSAL7 / 12

Fist of Five

Use it to: test the strength of agreement on one proposal and expose concerns quickly.

☝✌🤟🖐🖐

When to use

When the team needs to decide on a single idea, approach, experiment, or improvement action.

How to use

Define what each score means and what minimum agreement is needed. Everyone votes at the same time. Discuss low scores, adjust the proposal if needed, and vote again.

Real situation

“Should we stop carrying unfinished stories into the next Sprint?” Four people show strong support; one shows two fingers and explains a compliance dependency.

EXPLORE PERSPECTIVES8 / 12

Gallery Walk

Use it to: let people inspect ideas at their own pace, making different perspectives visible before the group discusses them.

Board 1Customer view
Board 2Team view
Board 3Risk view
Board 4Leader view

When to use

When several people or groups have produced material that others need to inspect without one presenter dominating.

How to use

Place outputs around the room or virtual space. Give people silent viewing time, then invite questions, observations, and patterns across the stations.

Real situation

“Each squad maps its biggest delivery risk on a board. Leaders walk through all boards before the group decides where support is needed.”

REFINE A LARGE SET9 / 12

White Elephant

Use it to: sort and refine a large set of items while building shared understanding of the categories.

Keep
Clear enough
Ready now
Clarify
Missing detail
Needs discussion
Challenge
Potentially wrong
Low value

When to use

When the group has a big unstructured set of ideas, stories, issues, or actions and needs to make sense of it together.

How to use

Create meaningful columns, place items visibly, and have participants move, question, and refine them until the group has a useful shared view.

Real situation

“Our backlog has 60 ‘quick wins’. The team sorts them into clear, unclear, duplicate, and discard — and suddenly sees that many are the same problem.”

OPEN OPTIONS, THEN NARROW10 / 12

Diverge → Converge

Use it to: avoid premature decisions by deliberately expanding options before choosing a path.

One problem
→
Many ideas
→
Few options
→
Decision

When to use

When the first idea is getting locked in too early or the team needs alternatives before committing.

How to use

First maximize useful possibilities without evaluating. Then cluster, test, compare, and narrow using explicit criteria.

Real situation

“The team wants to add another approval step. Before agreeing, they generate other ways to reduce risk and compare the options.”

DISTRIBUTE WEIGHTED CHOICE11 / 12

Thirty-Five

Use it to: let the group repeatedly compare ideas and distribute a fixed pool of points, making it harder for one voice to dominate.

Round 1
A ↔ BC ↔ D
Round 2
A ↔ CB ↔ D
Rounds 3–5
Re-mixRe-scoreTotal = 35

When to use

When many ideas seem important and you want a structured way to compare them repeatedly across the group.

How to use

Each person starts with a preferred idea. People work in pairs, distribute seven points between two ideas, swap partners, repeat for five rounds, then total the scores to 35.

Real situation

“We have six product ideas and no obvious winner. The repeated comparisons reveal which options hold up across different pairings.”

NEGOTIATE A SHARED PRIORITY12 / 12

Buy a Feature

Use it to: turn prioritisation into a negotiation where participants must combine limited resources rather than simply state preferences.

Feature A
₹60
Feature B
₹40
Feature C
₹80
Feature D
₹50
Limited budget → negotiate

When to use

When stakeholders have different priorities and you need a collaborative conversation about trade-offs.

How to use

Give participants limited play money. Price the options so nobody can buy everything alone. Let them negotiate and pool money to decide what matters most.

Real situation

“Product wants five features; engineering wants reliability work. The budget forces the conversation to become ‘what are we willing to trade away?’ rather than ‘my feature is most important.’”
