Go First: when a critical supplier dependency grounds the business

Go First filed for insolvency in May 2023 after saying engine availability had forced roughly half of its A320neo fleet out of service. The airline blamed Pratt & Whitney; the engine maker disputed responsibility. The systems lesson is dependency concentration: what happens when one external component limits the throughput of the entire business?

Live case2023IndiaAirlineSupplier dependency9–11 min
Capacity lossGo First said 25 aircraft—about 50% of its A320neo fleet—were grounded as of May 1, 2023.
Company-claimed impactThe airline said grounded aircraft had caused about ₹10,800 crore in lost revenue and additional expenses.
Financial exposureReuters later reported financial creditors were owed ₹65.21B (~$781M) as liquidation approached.
01 · THE SITUATION

A core asset could not operate without one constrained component

Go First's A320neo fleet depended on Pratt & Whitney geared-turbofan engines. The airline said the share of aircraft grounded because of engine failures rose over time until roughly half of its A320neo fleet was unavailable by May 2023. Pratt & Whitney disputed Go First's claims and pursued its own legal position.

Regardless of the disputed attribution, the operating-system problem is stark: an aircraft that cannot obtain a serviceable engine is effectively zero capacity while many of its costs continue. A dependency several layers outside the airline became a direct constraint on flights, revenue and cash flow.

Single critical dependencyThe fleet type depended on a specific engine ecosystem with limited near-term substitutes.
Fixed-cost exposureGrounded aircraft can still generate lease, financing, staffing and support costs.
Recovery lead timeEngine repair and spare-engine supply operate on long industrial lead times, not software-like deployment cycles.
Cash-flow loopLess flying means less revenue, which reduces the ability to finance recovery and absorb further disruption.
02 · DECISION POINT

Your critical supplier cannot restore enough capacity quickly. What do you optimize for?

Choose the trade-off you would defend when the bottleneck is outside your direct control.

The historical system could not restore sufficient capacity before cash pressure became critical.

Go First sought voluntary insolvency resolution in May 2023 and suspended flights. Its public statement attributed the move to engine failures and insufficient spare-engine availability; Pratt & Whitney disputed that attribution. What is undisputed is that a large portion of the fleet was grounded and the airline stopped operating.

03 · DEPENDENCY ECONOMICS

A dependency becomes existential when there is no practical substitute

Grounded capacity earns nothingUnavailable aircraft cannot generate passenger revenue even while the wider cost base continues.
Supplier lead time dominatesThe airline's recovery speed becomes bounded by an external production and maintenance system.
Concentration reduces optionalityA fleet optimized around one technology may become fragile when that technology's support network fails.
Cash amplifies the constraintAs operational throughput falls, financial capacity to create alternatives can fall too.
04 · OUTCOME

The airline stopped flying and entered insolvency

Go First's May 2023 statement claimed ₹10,800 crore in lost revenue and additional expenses from grounded aircraft. That figure is the airline's own claim, not an independently established final loss, so it should be labelled accordingly.

By August 2024, Reuters reported lenders had voted for liquidation after revival bids were rejected, with financial creditors owed ₹65.21 billion. Creditor exposure is also not the same as a final creditor loss; recoveries depend on insolvency outcomes.

05 · AGILE ORBIT LENS

Map dependencies before they become emergencies

Time to recoverResilience depends not only on failure probability but on how long replacement capacity takes.
Slack as insuranceSpare capacity looks inefficient until a constrained dependency fails.
Scenario planningModel what happens at 10%, 25% and 50% capacity loss before the event occurs.
