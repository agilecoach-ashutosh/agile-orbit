FBI Sentinel: recovering by shortening the learning loop

Sentinel was intended to modernize the FBI's investigative case-management system. After schedule, cost, usability and delivery problems, the FBI changed more than its project vocabulary: it assumed direct responsibility for development, reduced contractor dependence and used two-week Scrum sprints to complete the remaining work.

Live caseGovernmentProgram recovery9–11 minTurnaround
PressureA mission-critical modernization effort was late, costly and still incomplete.
ShiftThe FBI moved from prime-contractor-led development to direct management with a smaller team.
Learning loopTwo-week sprints connected users, developers and testers around demonstrable progress.
01 · THE SITUATION

Modernizing after an earlier failure

Sentinel was already operating under the shadow of a failed predecessor.

The FBI needed to replace its aging Automated Case Support environment with a modern electronic case-management and information-sharing system. Sentinel followed the earlier Virtual Case File effort, which the Department of Justice Office of the Inspector General described as an unsuccessful three-year, $170 million modernization effort.

Sentinel began as a large multi-phase program with Lockheed Martin as prime contractor. Early phases delivered some capability, but the program accumulated schedule pressure, cost growth and concerns about usability, performance and quality. In March 2010 the FBI issued a partial stop-work order for later phases.

By September 2010, the FBI faced an uncomfortable choice: continue trying to recover the existing delivery arrangement or change who owned the work and how progress was measured.

Mission criticalityThe system supported investigative workflow and information sharing; failure had operational consequences.
Legacy of failureThe prior Virtual Case File effort had already damaged confidence in large modernization programs.
Contract complexityDecision-making crossed FBI management, a prime contractor and multiple technology suppliers.
Weak progress signalsSchedule and phase completion did not always reveal whether usable, valuable software was actually ready.
02 · DECISION POINT

You inherit a late, high-visibility program with limited budget remaining. What changes first?

An independent assessment suggested the remaining work could take years and require substantial additional funding. The organization still needs the capability. You cannot simply abandon the mission.

There is no perfect option. Choose the path you would defend.

The FBI moved closest to Option B.

In October 2010 the FBI assumed direct control of Sentinel development, reduced Lockheed Martin's role and adopted an Agile approach. The OIG documented two-week Scrum sprints in which user and architecture stories were planned and completed, with working software treated as the primary measure of progress.

03 · WHAT CHANGED

The recovery altered ownership, team shape and the unit of progress

Calling the work “Agile” mattered less than changing the delivery system around it.

Direct FBI managementThe FBI assumed responsibility for managing development rather than relying on the prime contractor to carry the remaining program.
Smaller delivery organizationThe planned model reduced the number of contractor and FBI personnel involved, aiming to simplify decision paths.
Two-week sprintsShort iterations made working software and incomplete work more visible than long phase gates alone.
User + developer + tester collaborationThe OIG described Agile as close collaboration among system users, developers and testers around continuous delivery.
User stories + architecture storiesFunctional work and enabling architecture were both represented explicitly in the backlog.
Continuous oversight remainedAgile did not remove governance. OIG oversight continued to challenge dates, budgets, infrastructure readiness and operational costs.
04 · THE OUTCOME

A turnaround—without pretending the journey became easy

Recovery evidence is strongest when both success and remaining concerns are visible.

The FBI made Sentinel available to all users on July 1, 2012. The OIG reported an estimated total cost of $441 million, $10 million below the latest $451 million budget, while also noting that this figure excluded two years of operations and maintenance that had originally been included in the budget concept.

The OIG had earlier concluded that the transition to Agile improved the development process, yet it continued to flag concerns about revised deployment dates, hardware readiness and the fact that none of the Agile-developed functionality had yet been released to end users at that point. That nuance matters: short iterations improved transparency and delivery discipline, but they did not eliminate infrastructure, governance or deployment risk.

05 · COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“They switched to Scrum and everything worked” is the wrong lesson

COMMON MYTHSentinel succeeded because the FBI replaced waterfall with Scrum ceremonies.
→
MORE USEFUL LESSONThe recovery combined direct ownership, a smaller decision system, short feedback loops, closer user collaboration and continued oversight.
06 · AGILE ORBIT LENS

What this teaches about recovering troubled work

Working software as evidenceProgress becomes harder to hide when the system must produce completed, demonstrable functionality every short cycle.
Decision latencyReducing organizational layers can be as important as changing development mechanics.
Architecture is workArchitecture stories made enabling technical work explicit rather than pretending every backlog item was user-facing.
Governance ≠ phase gatesExternal oversight still mattered. Good governance asks for evidence, risk visibility and outcomes rather than only document completion.
07 · TRANSFER, DON'T COPY

Patterns for a program already under pressure

Problem signalCommon reactionMore adaptive move
Late large programCreate a more detailed master plan.Shorten the evidence cycle and demonstrate usable increments.
Too many partiesAdd coordination meetings and reporting layers.Clarify who owns delivery decisions and remove avoidable handoffs.
Requirements uncertaintyFreeze more detail earlier.Prioritize thin, valuable slices and learn from completed behavior.

08 · COACH THE SITUATION

Questions for a troubled program

ObserveWhat currently counts as “progress”—documents, spend, milestones, or working capability?
ChallengeWhich handoff exists mainly because accountability is fragmented?
ExploreWhat could users actually validate within the next two weeks?
ExperimentWhich small slice could prove both product value and an architectural assumption?
ReflectIf the plan is green but no usable capability is emerging, what is the dashboard hiding?
