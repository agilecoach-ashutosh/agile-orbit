TRANSFORM AT SCALE · BANKING

DBS: from product silos to customer journeys

DBS is often praised for becoming more digital. The more useful question is how a large regulated bank changed the way work was organized: journey thinking, experimentation, cross-functional squads and data-driven control towers created a management system aimed at customer outcomes rather than departmental output.

01 · THE SITUATION

Digital transformation was not just about digitising transactions

DBS deliberately framed transformation around how customers experience banking rather than how departments are organised.

DBS began using customer journey thinking and human-centred design years before its more recent operating-model changes. The bank described customer journeys as a way to understand what customers are actually trying to accomplish, then prototype and test improvements before scaling them.

It also pushed experimentation hard. DBS says it ran more than 1,000 experiments from 2015 onward, using agile and human-centred design approaches to learn early and cheaply. The important system implication is that experimentation only matters if the organisation can act on what it learns.

Product silosDepartments can optimize their own metrics while the customer experiences one end-to-end journey.
Slow learningLarge approval chains make experiments expensive and increase the cost of being wrong.
Regulated workRisk, controls and compliance must remain explicit even while delivery becomes faster.
ScaleThousands of people need a common management rhythm without one giant programme becoming a bottleneck.
02 · DECISION POINT

You want faster customer outcomes. What do you change first?

Assume technology investment is already significant. Customers still experience handoffs and delays across products and channels. Which move would you defend?

Choose the approach that best changes the learning system, not just the org chart.

DBS moved progressively toward Option C.

The bank built a culture of experimentation and journey thinking, then introduced Managing Through Journeys (MtJs): cross-functional squads guided by data-driven control towers and senior oversight. By 2023 DBS reported more than 60 journeys; its consumer bank said 98% of revenue was generated by MtJ squads comprising more than 2,000 people across technology, wealth and business teams.

03 · WHAT CHANGED

From departmental delivery to a journey-management system

The labels matter less than the feedback architecture behind them.

Customer journeys became the unit of thinkingTeams were encouraged to understand the customer's end-to-end job, not just one banking product or process step.
Cross-functional squadsBusiness, technology and specialist capabilities were brought closer around shared outcomes.
Data-driven control towersJourney performance was made visible so teams and leaders could react to customer pain points and business outcomes.
Experiments before scalePrototypes, hackathons and structured experiments reduced the cost of learning before committing large investments.
Senior ownershipLeadership oversight was strengthened as MtJs matured, linking priorities, outcome data and risk/control metrics.
Culture by designDBS invested in training and immersive methods rather than assuming a memo could create experimentation and customer focus.

04 · AGILE ORBIT LENS

What transfers beyond banking?

Copy the principles, not the DBS vocabulary.

Customer value cuts across silosIf an outcome requires five departments, optimizing each department independently will not optimize the journey.
Experimentation needs decision rightsAn experiment that cannot change priorities is theatre. Learning has value only when investment can move.
Data makes journeys governableAutonomy without observable outcomes becomes opinion. Control towers were a way of making customer and business signals visible.
Risk belongs in the systemRegulated agility works better when risk/control measures are integrated into the operating model instead of bolted on afterward.
05 · COACH THE SITUATION

Questions to take back to your organisation

ObserveWhere does one customer outcome cross the most teams, systems or approval boundaries?
ChallengeWhich local KPI could improve while the end-to-end customer journey actually gets worse?
ExperimentWhat is one journey you could manage with a shared outcome, a cross-functional team and weekly evidence for 90 days?
GovernWhich risk and control signals should be visible alongside value and speed metrics from day one?
